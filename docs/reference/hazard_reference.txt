Hazards are infinite
The following are examples of common hazards found in many buildings. This is not a comprehensive list—every building and inspection is different, and hazards can vary widely. In all cases, independent judgements and experience should be used to determine if a hazard is present and what action should be taken.
Window Air conditioning Units
Window air conditioning units are common and often installed incorrectly. Each unit should be bolted to the inside of the window frame or supported with metal brackets attached to the exterior wall. Without brackets, people often prop units with loose items such as bricks, wood, or books, which creates an added hazard if those items fall. When the AC units are removed, the loose items are often left on the sills, where they can fall or blow off. All air conditioners should have proper metal brackets, and there should be no loose items on exterior window sills.

Basement Hazards
Basements are common problem areas because they are rarely visited and often used for unwanted storage. They typically house building utilities, so problems often appear there first. Common issues include poor housekeeping, flooding, un-repaired ceiling leaks, hazardous activities (wood working, spray painting, cooking), laundry room hazards, electrical fire risks, and trip-fall hazards from poorly maintained stairways.

BBQ units
Barbecue units are popular but often used in unsafe areas. They should never be within 10 feet of a building or flammable structure, nor placed on fire escapes, balconies, or rooftops—unless professionally installed for that purpose. Tenant-purchased units should not be in or near buildings. Barbecues can be hard to spot since they are often mixed with other items or covered, but their silhouettes are usually recognizable even from a distance.

Exit doors and egress routes must remain clear of storage at all times. Because these doors are often rarely used, people tend to block them with stored items. This creates a serious hazard, especially at marked exits. Storage should never block doors, aisles, hallways, stairways, or stairwells—any walking area must stay unobstructed.

Bowstring Roofs
Wood bowstring roofs were once common in large spaces such as garages, malls, and bowling alleys. They have a straight span with a curved, barrel-shaped bow, creating a distinctive half-barrel roof. Hazards arise if the wood rots, connections loosen, or water intrusion occurs. In a fire, these roofs can fail catastrophically, leading to collapse. Ceiling Damage
Ceiling damage, especially to drop ceilings, is common and often overlooked. The panels themselves are easy to replace, but the real concern is what caused the damage. Water intrusion—whether from a roof leak, pipe condensation, or an upstairs water heater—is the underlying issue. Damaged ceilings should be repaired, but the source of the water must also be identified and corrected.

Compressed Gas Tanks
Compressed gas tanks, such as welding or medical oxygen tanks, present serious hazards. If a tank falls and its valve breaks, it can launch like a rocket, causing severe damage or death. Many gases are also flammable, adding fire risk. If compressed gas tanks are present on site it’s important to note how they are being used. For example, welding tanks indicate that welding work is being done and it’s important to understand that activity. Tanks must be stored with protective caps when not in use and secured tightly in an upright position with proper chains or straps. Chains or ropes that are too loose can still allow a tank to fall and don’t eliminate the issue. THey should also be stored so that they cannot be hit by forklifts or other machinery. Finally, tanks should be clearly labeled and flammable and non-flammable gases should be stored separately.

Commercial Cooking
Commercial cooking is a major fire source in commercial buildings. If a restaurant is present, underwriters need to know what is cooked, what equipment is used, and how it is protected. This applies even for liability coverage, since a restaurant fire endangers the entire building and its occupants, regardless of ownership. Common kitchen issues, beyond expired hood and AES tags, include several additional hazards listed below.
Fryers lacking baffles
Deep-fat fryers can cause fires if grease contacts open flames from nearby equipment. Each fryer must either have a permanently installed baffle separating it from open-flame appliances or be placed at a safe distance. Improvised barriers, such as baking trays, are not acceptable.

Floor frying and Pan frying
Deep-fat frying without a proper fryer—such as using pans on stoves or large pots on floor-mounted ranges—is hazardous. The same risks apply: flammable grease can ignite if it contacts open flames. Floor frying is especially dangerous due to the large volumes of hot grease, which can cause an immediate, severe fire if spilled or ignited. All significant frying should be done only in a dedicated deep-fat fryer.
Typical floor frying set up
Small counter top fryers
Some businesses without full kitchens use small countertop deep fryers behind counters or registers. These are hazardous because they produce flammable grease and vapors, often without proper hood or fire protection. Any countertop fryer requires a hood and a K-class fire extinguisher. Even small units count as commercial cooking and must be treated as such.

Inadequate hood systems and mesh filters
Some businesses cook or fry food using improvised or residential equipment. The key issue is grease-laden vapor, which is hazardous and accumulates on surfaces. Residential hood systems with mesh filters are acceptable at home but not in commercial settings. In any commercial setting, if cooking produces grease-laden vapors—or is more than simple boiling—a commercial hood system is required.

Decks and Railings
Exterior decks and railings often develop hazards from neglect. Wooden decks may rot or loosen at connections, making them unstable and prone to collapse. Railings can be unsafe if bolsters are spaced wider than four inches, missing, or built horizontally so children can climb them. These issues may be hard to spot without direct access but pose serious safety risks.

Electrical Hazards
Electrical systems always present fire risks and must be properly maintained. Several common hazards are listed below; these are not the only possibilities but are the ones most often encountered.
Gaps in breakers
Breaker panel gaps must be filled with approved blank breakers. Improvised covers such as cardboard or wood are not acceptable. Open gaps expose live components, creating shock and fire risks, and allow dirt or pests into the panel. Even a single gap suggests that there may be others  present in the property.

Tape on Breakers
It is unacceptable to use tape to hold a breaker open. This is usually an attempt to bypass tripping rather than fix the underlying problem. Taping a breaker indicates potential overloading or malfunction if causing the breaker to trip and that the breaker is being deliberately held open to prevent this. Breakers should only be locked open with purpose-built devices, such as in hospitals or industrial settings where critical systems must not be shut off. Any taped breaker should have the tape removed, and the panel inspected to ensure no damage has occurred and to identify the cause of tripping.

Missing Electrical Covers
Missing covers on breaker panels or junction boxes are common hazards. Often covers are removed during work and not replaced, or improvised panels are used instead of proper ones. All breakers and junction boxes must have covers designed for that equipment. Improvised or missing covers expose live wiring and create the same risks as breaker gaps—shock, fire, and panel failure.

Improvised wiring
All electrical work should be done by a licensed electrician, but this is often ignored to save money. Signs of improvised wiring include sloppy splices, extension cords or light bulbs wired into panels, and wires held with tape. These can be hard to detect, but unusual cords, fixtures, or wiring are warning signs. Any suspected improvised work should be inspected by a qualified electrician.

Fuses
Fuses—cartridge or screw-in—are a safe form of overcurrent protection, and cartridge fuses are still used in modern applications. However, older fuse panels, especially screw-in types, should be inspected by an electrician. The concern is not the fuses themselves but that they signal an aging electrical system, which may have other issues. An inspection ensures the system is still safe and functional. There should also not be anything in the fuse panel other than fuses. Pennys or other items can sometimes be used as a fill in for a fuse. These should be removed and replaced with fuses.

Knob and Tube Wiring
Knob and tube wiring, is an old style of wiring that uses ceramic knobs and tubes to support single-insulated copper wires. Though not inherently unsafe, its age, lack of grounding, and vulnerability to damage or DIY alterations increase risks of overheating and fire. Some older buildings, especially homes, may still have knob and tube wiring present. In all cases it should be inspected and replaced by an electrician.

Extension Cords
Extension cords are temporary solutions and should not power large appliances or serve as permanent wiring. Appliances such as freezers, refrigerators, or dryers must be plugged directly into wall outlets. Extension cords attached to ceilings, run permanently, or plugged into power strips indicate the need for additional outlets. Extension cords should never be used on a permanent basis.

Exterior hazards vary widely. Building owners and tenants often create issues outside, from fire pits too close to the structure to damaged decks, sinkholes, or deteriorating landscaping. Any exterior damage to the building or conditions that could cause someone to fall or be injured should be a concern, along with any conditions that could foreseeably cause these types of problems in the future. Fire Alarm systems
Fire alarm systems are either local or central station. Local systems only sound alarms within the building. Central station systems detect fires and automatically alert emergency services. It is often necessary to ask the building owner to confirm which type is present, though large systems with central panels are typically central station.

Fire Escapes
Not all buildings have fire escapes, but where present they must remain clear and unobstructed. Common problems include garbage, barbecues, or tenant belongings blocking them. Fire escapes may also be in poor condition—rusty, cracked, or damaged. They should be maintained and kept completely free of stored items.

Fire extinguishers are required in most buildings and occupancies. Each commercial unit, common area, and basement utility area should typically have one. Extinguishers must be wall-mounted with a visible tag showing a current inspection date. Fire doors are specially tested for fire resistance and must have a label stating their rating. The label is usually on the door edge, frame, or sometimes on the front or back. Without this label, the door cannot be confirmed as a fire door.

Graffiti
Graffiti on buildings falls into two categories. Commissioned murals are not an issue. Unapproved graffiti, especially gang-related or in hidden areas like alleys and hallways, is an attractive nuisance. It signals that areas are not monitored, encouraging more graffiti and unwanted activity. Any graffiti not clearly commissioned art should be removed.

Gutters
Gutters direct roof runoff to safe areas. Damaged or clogged gutters cause water to pool near foundations and create icy hazards in cold weather. Gutters should be intact, with downspouts directing water away from buildings and walkways, and cleaned regularly to prevent debris buildup.

Housekeeping
Housekeeping issues, such as excessive or misplaced storage, increase fire load and block spaces from being used properly. Storage must never block walking areas, egress routes, or roofs, and should be kept at least three feet from basement utility areas. Even organized but excessive storage—like large amounts of paper records creates additional fire risk. Disorganized storage, even if non-combustible, encourages further accumulation and should be removed or organized, since storage tends to grow over time if unchecked.

Laundry Rooms
Laundry rooms carry risks from water leaks and dryer lint fires. Hoses should be puncture-proof or armored, usually with a metal coating. Apartment laundry rooms require rigid metal dryer venting; flexible venting is only acceptable in single-family homes. Dryer filters must be cleaned regularly, and exterior vents checked to prevent lint buildup. A fire extinguisher should also be present in every apartment building laundry room. Large appliances, like dryers and washers should not be powered by extension cords.

Mold
Mold occurs when water enters a building and stagnates, often in drywall, insulation, or other permeable materials. It is less common on masonry. While only some molds are harmful, all mold should be treated as a concern since visual identification cannot confirm the type. Mold is usually obvious as black smears or circles, but a strong moldy odor—different from a mild basement mustiness—is also sufficient evidence, even if mold is not directly visible.

Playgrounds
Playgrounds vary widely in age and equipment, but common hazards include outdated or damaged equipment and inadequate ground cover. Swings should not exceed two per bay, and no S-hooks should be present. Ground cover—wood chips, rubber, etc.—must extend several feet around all equipment to cushion falls. Insufficient ground cover and outdated equipment are the most frequent issues.

Pushmatic Breakers
Pushmatic breakers are an old style of breakers. Usually they will say Pushmatic on them. They may have other labels. It's not a specific brand. It's a style. They look slightly different from other breakers because they're push buttons, not switches. Some underwriters are concerned about these because they may not be as effective. Others just want them to be flagged because they're an older system component and they indicate that the electrical system hasn't been updated in a long time. If Pushmatic breakers are present, there should be a recommendation to have an electrician come in and evaluate whether they're working properly and ensure that the system doesn't have any age-related issues. The main concern with these is that they are indicative of a system that has not been upgraded.

Roof hazards
Roofs are a major source of insurance claims. Leaks and damage to the roof surface are common, but even well-maintained roofs can fail if drains are blocked and water builds up, adding weight. Roofs should only be accessed for work or emergencies. The covering should be in good condition, regularly maintained, with no ponding water, blocked drains, or storage. If a roof is designed for tenant use, activity should stay within designated areas, and lightweight items such as plastic chairs should not be present, as they can blow off in high winds.
Ponding
Ponding is water buildup on a roof caused by poor drainage, blocked drains, or low spots from deterioration. Standing water is heavy and can damage or even collapse the roof. It also promotes algae and degrades the roof covering. Any ponding is a concern, especially large areas or ponding caused by blocked drains.

Storage
Storage on roofs creates multiple problems. Items can damage the roof surface, especially when heat softens materials like asphalt, leading to leaks. Stored objects can also blow off, endangering people or nearby buildings. Accessing stored items means more foot traffic, further damaging the roof. Roofs not designed for regular use should never have storage or debris. Leftover materials from roof work—such as buckets, wood, or tools—should always be removed.

Unauthorized access
Flat rooftops are often used by tenants as gathering spaces, but this is unsafe unless the roof is specifically designed for occupancy. Items such as barbecues, chairs, toys, or playground equipment should not be present. Only contractor-installed roof decks are acceptable, and even then, activity must be limited to the deck. No barbecues or lightweight items that could blow off should be allowed.

Deterioration
As roof coverings age and are exposed to the elements and UV rays and heat from sunlight, they will deteriorate. Different types of roof coverings deteriorate differently. Shingles will lose their granular covering, become deformed and blow off. Rolled asphalt will lose its granulation, metal will rust and rubber will wrinkle, blister, and fail at seams.
Many roof coverings will also develop moss or algae growth over time, which damages the surface
Roof coverings showing obvious age related issues should be inspected and or repaired by a roofer. Tarps of any kind on a roof are an obvious red flag as is water damage to ceilings on the upper floors of a building.

Sidewalks and Walkways
Sidewalks and exterior walking areas are common sources of trip-fall claims, making them a major concern for underwriters. Sidewalks should be in good condition, with even small cracks or vegetation flagged for review, since minor issues can worsen over time. This includes curbs and any exterior walking surface with cracks, heaving, or unevenness, all of which should be repaired by a qualified contractor. While responsibility for sidewalks may vary by jurisdiction, underwriters always want their condition documented. Even if maintenance is not the owner’s duty, liability claims often involve the building’s insurance.

Sprinkler Systems
A sprinkler system provides a high level of fire protection and it’s usually easy to confirm that there is some sort of sprinkler system present. For underwriters however, a simple confirmation that the system exists is not usually enough. In order to rate the building properly, they need to understand how much of the building the sprinkler system covers. For example, a building with sprinklers in the hallways only does not have the same protection level as an identical building with sprinklers in the hallways, basement, and apartments. For an underwriter, that is a big difference in terms of the rating.
System Coverage
Sprinkler heads need to be placed in more or less visible areas in order to be effective. The heads can either be exposed or behind cosmetic covers, but in either case they are usually easy to spot.Underwriters generally ask for photos of sprinkler heads from each area of the building that has sprinkler protection. So a building with sprinklers in the hallways and apartments would have photos unambiguously showing that sprinkler heads are present in those areas.  Sprinkler heads hidden behind cosmetic covers are designed to blend in and can be hard to spot at first.

Tools, spare heads and tags
Sprinkler systems are required to be annually inspected and to have inspection tags present. It’s also a requirement that there be spare sprinkler heads, and a tool for replacing sprinkler heads present. This is to ensure that any damaged heads can be quickly replaced, so that the system remains fully functional. Spare heads and tools are almost always kept in an easily identified rec box near the main valve, which is also where the tag should be located.

Securing the sprinkler main valve.
In addition to the spare heads, the wrench and the tags, sprinkler system main valves require additional safety measures to ensure that they cannot be easily closed. Closing the valve cuts of the the water supply to the sprinkler system and removes the fire protection to the building. Very often, if the sprinkler system does not stop a fire, it’s because it’s been shut off for some reason and not turned back on.

Fire Department Connections (FDC’s)
Some sprinkler systems and standpipe systems have a connection on the outside of the building that allows the fire department to pump additional water directly into the system. These connections have caps that must remain in place unless the fire department is using them. The caps prevent anything from entering the pipe and obstructing it, which could make it non-functional. Caps are often missing or get misplaced, and it’s also not uncommon for FDC’s that are missing caps to be stuffed with trash by passers by.

Freezing Hazards
A major cause of loss when it comes to sprinkler systems is freezing. A wet sprinkler system is essentially a network of water filled pipes, which will leak if it freezes. This usually  becomes an issue in vacant buildings where the heat has been shut off or turned very low and the sprinkler system has not been winterized or drained. In a case like this, a freeze can occur, and the pipes can leak for days before someone checks the building and notices the issue.
The other common cause of freeze damage is unusually low temperatures. Depending on the local climate, buildings may be designed with sprinkler systems in exterior walls or attics with a minimum of insulation. If temperatures drop below the minimum that the building is designed for, the pipes in the less protected areas can still freeze and leak even if the building heat is on.
In all cases, it’s important to communicate to the underwriter where the sprinklers are, and how they are kept warm enough to prevent freezing from occurring.

Other Common Issues
There are two other issues frequently seen with sprinkler systems, both having to do with the sprinkler heads and piping.
First, tenants will sometimes hang or attach things to the sprinkler pipes. This is especially prevalent in retail settings, where sprinkler pipes are a convenient mount for store items. Nothing should be attached to or hanging on sprinkler piping.
Second, tenants, especially in a warehouse or storage type setting, will store items too close to the sprinkler heads.This obstructs water flow if there is a fire, and the heads can be damaged if they are bumped by stored items. A bump like this usually causes the sprinklers to go off, causing a flood damage, or it can damage them, causing them to work less effectively.

Smoke Detectors
Smoke detectors, whether battery-powered, hardwired, or both, must be present in all apartment buildings and in some commercial buildings as well. Common issues include detectors missing from required areas or being removed by tenants. Tenants may take them down to avoid nuisance alarms or cover them with towels to disable them. Missing units can often be spotted by empty mounting brackets. Smoke alarm function should not be impeded in any way.

Stab-Lok Breakers
Stab-Lok breakers, manufactured by Federal Pacific Electric, are an older style known for defects that prevent them from tripping, creating serious fire hazards. Many have been replaced, but some panels remain and are always a concern for underwriting. Stab-Lok panels are identified by the Federal Pacific Electric label, the box shape, and breakers with orange (sometimes white) tabs. Other FPE components, like main shut offs, are not an issue—the concern is limited to the breakers. Any suspected Stab-Lok breakers should be flagged, inspected by an electrician, and likely replaced. Even if the panel does not say “Stab-Lok,” a panel resembling one is enough to recommend inspection.

Stairway Hazards
Falls on stairways are a major underwriting concern. Hazards include slippery treads, missing or inadequate handrails, and damaged or uneven steps. In general, any stairway with more than four risers should have a handrail; very wide stairways should have two. Treads must be uniform in height, free of damage, and non-slip or fitted with non-slip coverings. Handrails must be continuous, graspable, and free of gaps—wall-secured rails that prevent a full grip are not acceptable. Stairways must never be used for storage, whether small items like shoes or large items like boxes. Some common stairway issues are shown below. These are not a comprehensive list, just examples.
Missing Handrails
One very common issue is missing handrails on stairways that need them. Handrails may also be removed for some reason and not replaced.

Inadequate Handrails
Many stairways have handrails that are not fully graspable or that do not extend the full length of the stairway, or that have gaps in the handrail. These can be harder to spot because there is a handrail present, it’s just deficient in some way. All handrails should be continuous along the entire stairway and be fully graspable along their entire length.

Non-slip Treads
Many falls on stairs occur because the stairway surface is slippery. This can easily happen if the steps become wet, especially if the steps are made of a smooth polished stone like marble. Step surfaces should not be slippery under any conditions. Stone steps may require the addition of non-slip tread coverings to ensure that they do not become slippery. Damaged non-slip coverings pose an additional trip hazard and should be replaced with new ones.

Damaged Steps and Stairways
Older or poorly maintained stairways may have damage to various parts of the stairs. Usually the damage is to the steps and step treads or nosings, which can become worn and broken through regular use. Stairway steps and risers should be of a uniform height and free of damage or deterioration.

Structural issues are serious but usually easy to identify, as parts of the building may appear severely damaged or show unusual cracking. Any evidence of structural problems should be flagged immediately, as the underwriter will want to know right away.

Overhanging Trees
Overhanging trees create multiple hazards. They drop debris that clogs gutters and drains, damages roof surfaces, and causes leaks. Branches rubbing against roofs or walls in the wind can also cause damage. Dead trees or large dead limbs may fall on buildings or people. Trees should not overhang or touch buildings, roof and gutter debris should be cleared, and dead or dying trees near structures should be removed or trimmed back.

Water Heater Straps
Water heaters can topple during earthquakes, causing water damage or fire. In earthquake-prone areas such as California or Washington, they should be strapped upright and secured to walls or other structural supports to prevent tipping.

Zinsco Breakers
Zinsco breakers, also sold as Sylvania or GTE Sylvania, are an older brand with defects that prevent reliable tripping, creating fire hazards similar to Stab-Lok breakers. Any Zinsco breakers found should be inspected and replaced by an electrician. They are easy to spot by their color-coded breakers and labels such as Zinsco, GTE Sylvania, or Sylvania Zinsco. Other Zinsco components are not necessarily problematic but may indicate Zinsco breakers elsewhere in the building. The breakers are the real concern as those are the components that are known to have a defect.
